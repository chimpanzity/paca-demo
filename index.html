In 2004, Lee, Conroy, McGreevy & Barraclough built an adaptive opponent to study decision-making in macaques: an algorithm that watches every choice you make, runs nine statistical pattern detectors over your history, and exploits whatever regularity it finds. Dodging Proteus (Martin, 2026) put seven language models against it for 200-trial sessions of matching pennies — and found that prescribing the correct equilibrium strategy often made a model play worse.

This site recreates that contest as an arcade battle. You (or a model, replayed verbatim from the study's released data) command a missile battery; Proteus commands the countermeasures. Match its lane and you're intercepted — slip past it and score. The only unbeatable strategy is a fair coin. Can you be random?

How the arcade maps to matching pennies

  • Left / right lane = the two actions (Triangle / Star in the lab task).
  • Proteus commits its move from history before your choice — a true simultaneous game.
  • Interception = Proteus matched you. Breakthrough = you mismatched it. Nash equilibrium is an i.i.d. fair coin, worth 50% in expectation; every departure from it is a detectable, exploitable cost.
  • The TARGET LOCK reticle appears only after you commit, so it can't inform your choice — it shows you, mid-flight, that a channel (side bias, choice history, or choice+outcome history) had reached significance.
  • The post-game debrief applies the paper's diagnostics: win rate vs the .50 benchmark (Fig 3A), marginal balance with an i.i.d. reference band (3B), runs-test z for sequential dependency (3C), and the Brown–Rosenthal stay/shift policy square (Fig 4).
Fine print: in the lab task the model played the matcher role (it won by matching Proteus); the arcade flips the roles so the human plays the intuitive “get past the defender” seat. Replays mirror Proteus's displayed lane accordingly — a pure relabeling that preserves every statistic. Also, spatial left/right choice may recruit different biases than abstract symbol choice, so treat the arcade as an illustration of the assay, not a port of the experiment.
